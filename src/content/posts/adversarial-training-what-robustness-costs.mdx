The paper makes a clean claim. Train against the worst perturbation you can find
inside an epsilon ball, and you get a model that holds up inside that ball.
Projected gradient descent is the strong first-order adversary, so it is what
you evaluate against. That second half is why "adversarial accuracy" now means
"accuracy under PGD" by default.

What the paper does not settle is what the defence costs you, and whether you
can keep most of the robustness while paying less of it. That is what my MSc
dissertation measured: a ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, three training regimes, every
hyperparameter held fixed across all three so the comparison could not drift,
then attacked with FGSM and with PGD at epsilon 8 over thirty iterations.

The clean-trained baseline is the best model in the set on the test set, 91.58%,
and it scores 0.57% under PGD. Ten classes, so that is worse than guessing.
The two adversarially trained models give up between 2.83 and 5.77 points of
clean accuracy and come back with 35 to 39 points under attack, a factor of 68
on the baseline.

Nothing there contradicts the paper, but it lands differently when the numbers
are yours. Ranked on clean accuracy the models go v1, v3, v2. Ranked under
attack they go v2, v3, v1. The order reverses completely, so a single reported
accuracy figure gets the answer exactly backwards.

## The hybrid schedule lost, and it was mine

v3 was the proposed contribution: 50 clean epochs then 50 adversarial ones,
rather than adversarial throughout. The argument was that you could hold on to
clean accuracy without conceding much robustness.

It does the first half. 88.75% clean is 2.94 points above fully adversarial
training. It also hands back 3.09 points under PGD, 35.66 against v2's 38.75.
So it trades at close to one for one, and if the attacker is real, v2 is the
model to pick. The dissertation's own conclusion reads more warmly toward the
hybrid than these three numbers support, which is the main reason I am writing
it up again.

## FGSM flatters everyone

Both adversarially trained models were trained on FGSM at epsilon 2, so their
FGSM scores are partly a result about their own training distribution. The
repository reports FGSM as a band across epsilon rather than per step: 16.84%
to 61.31% for the baseline, 46.01% to 76.00% for v2, 43.78% to 77.30% for the
hybrid. Even the undefended model survives a single gradient step reasonably
often. PGD is where the gap between "trained against this attack" and "robust"
becomes visible.

Grad-CAM agrees with the accuracy numbers. Saliency on v2 and v3 spreads over
broader and more relevant regions of the image than on v1. That is the only
qualitative claim here, so I am not leaning on it.

## What this does not show

One architecture, one dataset, one training epsilon and one test epsilon.
No PGD-trained model, which is the obvious missing cell: everything here is
trained on FGSM and evaluated under PGD, so these robustness figures are a
generalisation result rather than the best the method can do. The whole study
ran on Leeds' ARC4 cluster in MATLAB, and the model-by-attack matrix, all nine
cells of it, is in the
[repository](https://github.com/rajo69/Enhancing-Neural-Network-Robustness-using-Hybrid-Adversarial-Training).
