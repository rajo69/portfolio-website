The original paper establishes something useful: putting a sigmoid gate after
attention makes language models better. It reports aggregate statistics about
those gates, layer-wise means and attention sink percentages, then stops there.

What it does not answer is the question I care about. Once training is done,
what are the individual gates doing, and what breaks if you interfere with
them? So I took the released pretrained models and ran forward hooks, layer
ablations and cross-domain checks over 25,600 WikiText-2 tokens. No training,
no improvement claims, just measurement of a model somebody else trained.

## The gates are close to binary

The first surprise is that gate dimensions are not spread across a continuum.
They cluster at the ends.

<Bars
  caption="Elementwise gate dimensions by behaviour, aggregated with Welford over 25,600 WikiText-2 tokens. The categories overlap, so they do not sum to 100. Split-half stability confirmed."
  data={[
    { label: "Stably suppressed", value: 48.3, series: 0 },
    { label: "Dynamic", value: 54.8, series: 1 },

Roughly half the dimensions are held down essentially all the time, and a
slightly larger share genuinely vary with the input. The two sets overlap,
which is why the numbers do not add to a hundred.

## Layer 0 is doing nearly all the work

The more striking result is how unevenly the damage is distributed when you
start opening gates.

<Bars
  caption="Share of total ablation damage attributable to layer 0 versus every other layer combined. Bootstrapped confidence intervals, consistent with the layer sparsity pattern."
  data={[
    { label: "Layer 0", value: 99.8, series: 0 },
    { label: "All other layers", value: 0.2, series: 1 },
  ]}
  max={100}

Ablating layer 0's gate accounts for 99.8% of the perplexity damage. Everything
else is rounding error. My first assumption was a bug in the harness, and that
is most of why the confidence grades in the repository exist.

## What did not work

I expected forcing layer 0's gate open to restore the attention sink. It does
not. That is a null result and I have graded it moderate: the attention weight
hooks are doing what I think they are doing, but the mechanism is unclear and
it does not contradict the paper's system-level claim.

Related, the best fixed gate value I could find, 0.15, is still 99.21
perplexity worse than the learned gates. Whatever the gates are encoding, a
constant does not approximate it.

## What this does not show

One model size. One primary dataset. No retraining. The finding that some heads
show no quality loss when killed is graded low for exactly that reason, and it
stays low until it has been run against another benchmark.

The full findings table, with a confidence grade and the evidence behind every
row, is in the repository, along with the `.pkl` files needed to reproduce the
whole thing on a free Colab T4 in about three hours.
